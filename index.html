Behind the game

Who are you
training for?

I wanted to catch the moment a workout stops being your idea. Maybe you meant to train legs today. Then a judge compliments your arms. What do you click next?

The mirror makes every change visible. The scorecard tells you which changes get applause. After the final, you can hand the deciding vote to someone else and watch the same three bodies get judged again.

A study behind the mirror

In a study of 58 women who were generally inactive, those who exercised facing a mirror reported feeling worse afterward than those who exercised facing a plain wall. The researchers found no effect on exercise self-efficacy. That distinction helped shape the game’s question about how it feels to be watched.

Martin Ginis, Jung, and Gauvin, Health Psychology 22 (2003), pp. 354–361.

How the scores work

Brass awards more impact points as the most-trained group grows. Lyra awards more balance points as the gap between the most- and least-trained groups shrinks. Each judge gives up to 100 points. The head judge counts twice in the average. Athletes tied for first share the title and the trip home.
